TOPIC: Millets, Cooking Methods, and Fat Intake in the Indian Diet

Millets and whole grains:
Millets such as jowar (sorghum), bajra (pearl millet), ragi (finger millet), and other traditional grains are encouraged as a regular part of the Indian diet alongside rice and wheat. Millets are generally higher in fiber, iron, and calcium than polished rice or refined wheat flour (maida), and they have a lower glycemic impact, which can help with blood sugar management. Mixing millets into daily meals — for example, using ragi or jowar flour for rotis, or millet-based khichdi — is an easy way to increase whole grain intake.

Cooking oils and fat intake:
Fat is an essential part of the diet but should be consumed in moderation. The guidelines recommend:
- Using a variety of cooking oils over time (mustard oil, groundnut oil, sesame oil, rice bran oil) rather than relying on one single oil, since different oils offer different fatty acid profiles.
- Limiting deep-fried foods and heavily ghee-laden dishes, especially for regular daily meals; reserve these for occasional consumption.
- Preferring cooking methods like steaming, boiling, roasting, and light sautéing over deep-frying.
- Being mindful of hidden fats in processed and packaged snacks, bakery items, and fried street foods, which are common in urban Indian diets.

Reducing salt and processed food intake:
Traditional Indian diets often include high-sodium items such as pickles (achaar), papad, chutneys, and salted snacks (namkeen). While these can be enjoyed occasionally, regular high intake contributes significantly to excess sodium consumption. Reducing quantities of these items, and choosing fresh home-cooked meals over packaged/processed foods, helps align with the recommended daily salt limit of under 5g.
